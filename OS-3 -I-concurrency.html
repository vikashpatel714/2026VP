OPERATING SYSTEM (OS) · UNIT: CONCURRENCY & PROCESS SYNCHRONIZATION

Many processes, one CPU.
How the Operating System stops the crash.

A modern computer runs hundreds of processes that all want the CPU and shared memory at once — like trains sharing one rail line. The Operating System is the signalman: it uses tools like locks, semaphores, and monitors to guarantee mutual exclusion in the critical section. Every OS concept below comes with a real-world example and a small working simulation you can operate yourself, so you can watch a race condition happen — and watch the OS fix it.

01 Introduction to Concurrency

What does it mean for things to happen "at the same time"?

Concurrency is what happens when more than one process is alive and making progress during the same stretch of time — even if, underneath, the CPU is secretly switching between them very fast.

On a single-core computer, only one instruction can actually execute at any instant. The operating system creates the illusion of many things happening together by slicing time into tiny turns and rapidly switching the CPU between processes — this is called time-sharing. On a multi-core computer, genuinely different processes can run on different cores at the exact same instant — this is parallelism. Concurrency is the broader idea that covers both: it is about structure — many independent activities in progress — while parallelism is about physically doing them simultaneously.

IdeaWhat's really happening
ConcurrencyMultiple tasks make progress in overlapping time windows (may or may not be simultaneous)
ParallelismMultiple tasks execute at the exact same physical instant, on separate cores
REAL LIFE — ONE COUNTER, MANY CUSTOMERS

Think of a single railway ticket counter with one clerk. The clerk serves customer A for a few seconds, pauses, switches to customer B, comes back to A — nobody actually waited in one unbroken queue, yet by the end of the hour it looks like everyone was served "together." That's concurrency on a single core. Now imagine the station opens a second counter with a second clerk — now two customers really are being served at the same literal moment. That's parallelism.

Types of process

INDEPENDENT PROCESS

Does not share data with any other process, and its result does not depend on anyone else's progress. Example: you opening a calculator app while music plays in the background — neither one needs to know the other exists.

COOPERATING PROCESS

Shares data with other processes and can affect, or be affected by, them. Example: a video call app has one process capturing your camera and another sending that data over the network — they must constantly hand data to each other. Cooperating processes are exactly where concurrency problems (like race conditions) are born.

02 Race Condition

When two processes race to touch the same variable

A race condition happens when the final result depends on the unpredictable timing of how two processes interleave — and different timings give different (wrong) answers.

Suppose two threads both run balance = balance + 100. That single line is actually three CPU steps: read the current balance, add 100 to it, then write it back. If Thread A reads the balance, and before it writes back, Thread B also reads the same (still old) balance — both threads compute from the same starting number. One update quietly overwrites the other, and 100 rupees vanish into thin air.

REAL LIFE — THE SHARED BANK BALANCE

Two ATM machines both read your balance as ₹1,000 at nearly the same second. Each adds a ₹100 deposit and writes ₹1,100 back. You deposited ₹200 total, but your balance shows only ₹1,100 instead of ₹1,200 — one deposit was lost because neither ATM knew about the other's half-finished work.

03 Critical Section

The one room only one process may enter

The critical section is the part of a program's code that touches shared data. To avoid races, an OS must guarantee that only one process is ever inside it at a time.

Any correct solution to the critical-section problem must satisfy three rules:

RequirementMeaning
Mutual exclusionIf one process is inside the critical section, no other process may enter
ProgressIf no one is inside, and some processes want to enter, the decision of who goes next cannot be postponed forever
Bounded waitingThere is a limit on how many times other processes may enter before a waiting process gets its turn
REAL LIFE — THE SINGLE CHANGING ROOM

A shop has one trial room and many customers. Mutual exclusion: only one shopper is inside with the door locked. Progress: if the room is empty, the shop cannot make the next person wait for no reason. Bounded waiting: the shop can't let the same three people cut the queue forever while someone else never gets a turn.

04 Inter-Process Communication

How separate processes talk to each other

Processes normally live in their own private memory. IPC is the set of mechanisms an OS offers so cooperating processes can exchange data safely.

Shared Memory

The OS maps one block of physical memory into both processes' address spaces. Both can read and write it directly — it's fast, but the processes themselves must synchronize access to avoid a race condition, since the OS does not police it for them.

LIKE

A shared whiteboard in an office — anyone can walk up and write on it any time, so the team has to agree on rules ("only one person writes at once") themselves.

Message Passing

Processes exchange data by explicitly sending and receiving messages through the OS (pipes, sockets, message queues). Slower than shared memory, but naturally synchronized — a message cannot be received before it is sent.

LIKE

Sending a letter through the post office instead of shouting across the whiteboard — slower, but the postal system guarantees delivery order and no one else can scribble on your letter mid-transit.

05 Producer–Consumer Problem

A factory belt with a fixed number of crates

A producer keeps making items and a consumer keeps using them — but they share one buffer of limited size. The producer must not add to a full buffer; the consumer must not remove from an empty one.

REAL LIFE — BAKERY CONVEYOR BELT

A baker places fresh loaves on a conveyor belt with only 6 slots. If all 6 slots are full, the baker must wait before placing another loaf. A shopkeeper takes loaves off the belt to sell; if the belt is completely empty, the shopkeeper must wait for a fresh loaf. Three semaphores keep this safe: empty counts free slots, full counts filled slots, and mutex makes sure only one of them touches the belt at any instant.

Notice: "Producer: add item" does nothing once empty reaches 0, and "Consumer: remove item" does nothing once full reaches 0 — the semaphores are physically blocking the wrong move.

06 Process Synchronization

Five ways to build the lock on the door

Over the years, several techniques were invented to guarantee mutual exclusion. Each fixes a flaw in the one before it.

Lock variable (the naive attempt)

The simplest idea: keep one shared variable lock. Before entering the critical section, check if lock == 0, then set it to 1; on exit, set it back to 0. The flaw is that "check" and "set" are two separate steps — exactly the two-step gap that causes a race condition, just one level up. Two processes can both see lock == 0 before either has set it to 1, and both barge in together.

REAL LIFE

A meeting room with a paper sign you flip to "Occupied" yourself. Two people can glance at the door, both see "Vacant," and both start walking in before either flips the sign — the sign only works if checking and flipping happen as one uninterruptible action.

Strict alternation

Two processes share one variable turn. Process 0 may enter only when turn == 0, and must set turn = 1 on exit — and vice versa. This does guarantee mutual exclusion, but it forces strict ping-pong turns even when one process has nothing to do. If P0 finishes its work instantly and P1 is slow, P0 must still sit idle waiting for a turn it doesn't need — this violates the progress requirement.

Peterson's Solution

Peterson's solution fixes both flaws above using two tools together: a flag[i] for each process ("I want to enter") and a shared turn variable used only as a tie-breaker. A process sets its own flag, then politely says turn = other — "you go first if we both want in." It waits only while the other process's flag is up and it is genuinely the other's turn. This satisfies mutual exclusion, progress, and bounded waiting for two processes.

Click both requests back to back — only one lane turns green at a time, and it's always decided by the turn tie-breaker.

Lamport's Bakery Algorithm

Peterson's trick only works for two processes. Lamport's Bakery algorithm generalizes it to any number of processes using an idea straight from an actual bakery counter: every process picks a ticket number one higher than the highest number currently out, then waits until its number is the smallest among everyone waiting (ties broken by process ID). Whoever holds the lowest number is served next.

Counter is empty. Add a few processes, then serve them — always lowest ticket first.

Test-and-Set Lock (hardware instruction)

Modern CPUs solve the "check-then-set" race directly in hardware with an atomic instruction: TestAndSet(lock) reads the old value of lock AND sets it to 1, as one indivisible step that cannot be interrupted. A process spins in a tight loop calling TestAndSet until it gets back false (meaning the lock was free and is now theirs). This is simple and correct, but wastes CPU time — this is called busy waiting, or a spinlock.

07 Semaphores & Monitors

A signal, a key, and a self-locking room

Semaphores are named after the railway signal in our hero banner above — a shared signal that tells processes whether it's safe to proceed.

A semaphore is an integer variable, accessed only through two atomic operations: wait() (also called P, decrements the value — used to "ask for a slot") and signal() (also called V, increments the value — used to "release a slot"). If wait() would take the value below what's allowed, the calling process is put to sleep instead of busy-waiting, which is more efficient than a spinlock.

TypeBehaviour
Counting semaphoreValue can range over any non-negative integer — used to manage a pool of N identical resources
Binary semaphoreValue is only 0 or 1 — behaves like a simple mutex lock, used for pure mutual exclusion
REAL LIFE — THE PARKING LOT BOOM BARRIER

A parking lot has 5 spaces. A counting semaphore starts at 5. Each car entering calls wait(), dropping the count; the barrier only lifts if the count is still positive. Each car leaving calls signal(), raising the count and freeing a space for the next car. A binary semaphore is the same idea shrunk to one single reserved space, like a single shared office key: whoever holds the key locks everyone else out until they hang it back up.

Monitor

Semaphores work, but it's easy for a programmer to forget a wait() or signal() somewhere and break everything silently. A monitor is a higher-level, safer construct: it's a module that bundles shared data together with the only procedures allowed to touch that data, and the language/runtime itself guarantees that only one process may be active inside the monitor at a time — the programmer never has to remember to lock or unlock anything by hand. When a process inside the monitor finds it can't proceed, it calls wait() on a condition variable and steps outside to sleep; another process later calls signal() to wake it.

REAL LIFE — THE KITCHEN WITH ONE DOOR

A restaurant kitchen has exactly one door and a rule: only one cook may be inside at a time, enforced by the building itself, not by the cooks remembering to lock anything. If a cook goes in and finds no onions, they don't stand there blocking everyone — they step out and wait on a bench (a condition variable) until someone signals "onions have arrived," then walk back in.

08 Classical Problem

The Dining Philosophers

Five philosophers sit around a round table. Between every pair of neighbours lies exactly one fork — five forks in total. To eat, a philosopher needs both forks on either side of their plate. Otherwise, they just think.

REAL LIFE — THE DEADLOCK EVERY DINNER TABLE FEARS

Imagine every single philosopher picks up their left fork at exactly the same moment. Now every fork is held, and every philosopher is stuck waiting for the fork on their right — which their neighbour is holding and refusing to put down until they've eaten. Nobody can move. This exact situation is called a deadlock: everyone is stuck waiting on everyone else, forever. The classic fix is simple: let at most n-1 philosophers (here, 4 out of 5) try to sit down and pick up forks at the same time — with one seat always free, the circular wait can never close completely.

09 Classical Problem

The Readers–Writers Problem

Many processes share one document. Any number of readers may look at it together safely — but a writer needs the document completely to itself, with nobody else reading or writing.

REAL LIFE — A SHARED GOOGLE-STYLE DOCUMENT

Twenty people can have a shared document open and read it at once with zero problems — reading doesn't change anything, so it's always safe to share. But the moment someone wants to edit it, everyone else must be locked out of both reading and editing until the edit finishes, otherwise a reader could see a half-written sentence, or two writers could overwrite each other's changes.

10 Classical Problem

The Sleeping Barber

A tiny barbershop has one barber, one barber chair, and 3 waiting chairs. If there are no customers, the barber sleeps. A customer either wakes the barber, waits, or leaves if the shop is full.

REAL LIFE — YOUR NEIGHBOURHOOD BARBERSHOP

No point paying a barber to stand around when nobody's there — so he naps (this models a semaphore putting an idle process to sleep instead of wasting CPU on busy-waiting). A new customer either finds him asleep and wakes him, finds him busy and takes a free waiting chair, or finds every waiting chair full and walks out to find another shop — exactly like a bounded buffer of size 3 rejecting new items once it's full.
